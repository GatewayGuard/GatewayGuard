# SECTION 7 -- Reviewing the Programs on Your PC

## 7a -- what the feature is

> **AS IT STANDS:** *"Checkup reviews the programs installed on your PC and
> points out any we consider risky, with a plain-English explanation of why we
> think so."*

> **REMOVED:** the section is titled *"Reviewing the Programs on Your PC"* and
> then **never says what that is.** It opens instead with *"Checkup never
> removes a program on its own"* -- a limit on a feature the reader has not
> been told about.

> **FLATTENED:** *"Checkup lists the programs installed on your PC and
> identifies any we consider risky, with our reasons."*

**Cost of removing outright: the section loses its subject.** *"plain-English
explanation"* is the sales language; *"with our reasons"* is the term.

## 7b -- the approval record

> **AS IT STANDS:** *"Checkup never removes a program on its own. If you want
> a program removed, you approve that removal on screen, and Checkup records
> your approval with the date and time in your log file."*

> **REMOVED:** **nothing in the agreement then says a removal needs your
> approval.**

> **FLATTENED:** *"Checkup does not remove any program unless you approve it
> on screen. Your approval is recorded in your log file with the date and
> time."*

**Cost of removing outright: high, and it is the one I would argue hardest
about.** This is the product's central promise written as a contract term, and
it is the paragraph that protects **us** -- it is the agreed record that the
customer chose the removal. **The warmth is in the word "never"; the substance
is the sentence after it.** The flattened version keeps everything that
matters.

# SECTION 8 -- What GatewayGuard Does Not Promise

**Read this before the three rows.** The section opens: *"Except for the
promises in Section 6, Checkup and the Guide are provided 'as is.'"*

**The three tagged paragraphs are what that phrase actually means.** A general
"as is" is a short phrase a reader skims. The specific sentences are what put
the buyer on notice about the particular thing they might later complain
about. ***inferred, and this is exactly the question to put to the attorney:***
**a specific disclaimer usually does more work than a general one**, and
consumer agreements are read against the party that wrote them.

## 8a -- no promise of immunity

> **AS IT STANDS:** *"We do not promise that Checkup will make your computer
> immune to every security threat, catch every piece of malware, or prevent
> every attack. No security tool can promise that."*

> **REMOVED:** a **security product** sells itself with no sentence anywhere
> saying it cannot stop everything.

> **FLATTENED:** *"Checkup cannot make your PC immune to every threat or catch
> every piece of malware. No security product can."*

**Cost of removing outright: the highest of the three, and I would not do
it.** This is the disclaimer a disappointed customer's complaint lands on.
**The last five words are the only part that reads as marketing**, and the
flattened version keeps them because they are also true and they are the
reason.

## 8b -- no promise about every PC

> **AS IT STANDS:** *"We do not promise that Checkup will work correctly on
> every possible combination of hardware and software."*

> **REMOVED:** **and this one leaves a contradiction behind.** Section 6's
> promises are limited to *"PCs with hardware not listed on our compatible PC
> list"* -- which implies a list exists and is the boundary. Without 8b there
> is no general statement that unusual machines may simply not work.

> **FLATTENED:** *"Checkup may not work correctly on every combination of
> hardware and software."*

**Cost of removing outright: moderate.** **This is the shortest and least
marketing-flavoured of the six** -- I am not sure why it was tagged, and if
the tag was a sweep rather than a judgement, this is the one to leave alone
entirely.

## 8c -- settings may not survive Windows updates

> **AS IT STANDS:** *"We do not promise that the settings you approved will
> stay in place after Windows updates. Microsoft releases a major Windows 11
> update most years, and those updates may change or reset security settings.
> We recommend running Checkup again after each yearly Windows update so you
> can see what changed and approve any settings you want turned back on."*

> **REMOVED:** **this is the one with a commercial consequence, and it is
> worth noticing before deciding.** That paragraph is where the agreement
> states *why* a yearly Windows update matters -- which is the stated basis
> for the annual updated version being **a separate purchase**. Cut it, and
> the separate-purchase term stands on its own with no reason given.

> **FLATTENED:** *"Windows updates can change or reset security settings, so
> we do not promise the settings you approved will stay in place. Run Checkup
> again after a yearly Windows update to see what changed."*

**Cost of removing outright: it removes the reason for the annual product.**
**The last sentence is not marketing -- it is the instruction a customer needs
in order to stay protected.** The flattened version is two sentences instead
of three.

# SECTION 9 -- Refunds

## 9a -- the Guide is sold without a refund

> **AS IT STANDS:** *"The Guide -- sold without a refund. Every security
> setting the Guide covers is explained free on our website, in full, before
> you buy anything. You can read the whole of what the Guide covers and decide
> if the offline copy is worth it to you. Because you can see exactly what you
> are getting beforehand, the Guide is sold without a refund from us."*

> **REMOVED:** what is left is **"the Guide is sold without a refund"** and
> nothing else.

> **FLATTENED:** *"The Guide is sold without a refund. Everything it covers is
> explained free on our website before you buy, so you can see exactly what
> you are getting."*

## THIS IS THE ONE I WOULD NOT REMOVE IN ANY FORM

**The paragraph is not decorating the term. It is the argument for it.**

A bare no-refund term on a digital product is the kind of clause consumer
regulators look at. **What makes ours defensible is the fact stated in the
middle of it: the entire contents are readable free, in full, before any money
changes hands.** That is genuinely unusual and genuinely fair, and **the
agreement is the place that fact is on the record.**

***And it is load-bearing inside our own project:*** `CLAUDE.md` records that
the Guide's no-refund rule leans on this paragraph directly.

> **Remove the reason and you keep the term while discarding the defence of
> it.** That is the opposite of the trade you want.

**The flattened version cuts it from four sentences to two and loses nothing
that matters.**

# FOR THE ATTORNEY -- THREE QUESTIONS THIS RAISES

These go into the consult list:

1. **Does the specific "we do not promise" wording in Section 8 do more work
   than the general "as is" in its opening line?** If not, the tone problem
   solves itself by deletion.
2. **Section 9: does stating the reason for the Guide's no-refund term
   strengthen it?** If the term stands or falls on its own regardless, that
   changes what belongs in the agreement and what belongs on the website.
3. **Section 7b: is the log-file record of a customer's on-screen approval
   worth anything as evidence** if a customer later says they did not agree to
   a removal? **If it is, the wording should probably be firmer than it is
   now, not softer.**
