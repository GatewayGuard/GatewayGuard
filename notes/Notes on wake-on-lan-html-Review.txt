Phrases like **"whether"** and **"that is"** (as well as filler phrases like *“the fact that,” “in terms of,”* and *“as to whether”*) are almost always wordy padding that add no actual meaning to a sentence. Removing them tightens your writing, makes your points sharper, and improves readability immediately.

Here is how you can easily eliminate them without losing any context:

### 1. Eliminating "Whether"

When used in phrases like *“the question of whether”* or *“whether or not,”* the word is almost always dead weight.

* **Wordy:** The system checks **whether** the box is ticked.
* **Concise:** The system checks if the box is ticked. *(Or even better: The system checks if the box is ticked.)*
* **Wordy:** GatewayGuard asked **whether** to turn it off.
* **Concise:** GatewayGuard asked to turn it off. *(Or: GatewayGuard asked if you wanted to turn it off.)*

### 2. Eliminating "That is"

"That is" (and its sibling *“which is”*) frequently traps writers into creating passive, bloated clause structures when a simple modifier or direct noun will do.

* **Wordy:** Remote Desktop and Wake on LAN are the two features **that are** designed to let someone in.
* **Concise:** Remote Desktop and Wake on LAN are two features designed to let someone in.
* **Wordy:** It is a real need—**that is**, in an office.
* **Concise:** It is a real need in an office.

---

### A Quick Before-and-After Example

> **Wordy Draft:**
> The main issue is **whether** your computer is set up to wake up remotely, **that is**, via a network signal **that is** sent from another machine.

> **Tightened Version:**
> The main issue is whether your computer is set up to wake up remotely via a network signal sent from another machine.

*(For  "whether" can often be swapped for "if" or cut entirely depending on the phrasing.)* 

Trimming these fillers makes your writing punchier and respects your reader's time.
