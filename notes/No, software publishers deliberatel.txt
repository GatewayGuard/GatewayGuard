No, software publishers deliberately structure EULAs to **avoid** having to amend or reissue the legal agreement for minor software updates, patches, or version increments.

To achieve this flexibility, companies typically use specific legal mechanisms so the contract remains valid across multiple iterations of a product:

* **Broad Product Definitions:** The EULA often defines the "Software" broadly to include the currently downloaded application "together with all updates, bug fixes, patches, modifications, enhancements, and replacement versions" provided by the publisher. Because updates are contractually baked into the definition of the product from the start, delivering a new patch doesn't legally create a new agreement.
* **Version-Independent Language:** Instead of hardcoding version numbers (like *v1.0* or *v2.4*) into the main body of the contract, agreements usually refer to the product by its general trade name or trademark. Version specifics, system requirements, and supported operating systems are relegated to external documentation like release notes, read-me files, or website specifications, which can be updated instantly without altering the underlying legal text.
* **General Update Mechanisms:** Most EULAs include a "Changes to Terms" or "Modifications" clause. If a major architectural shift occurs—such as introducing a new cloud-connected telemetry feature, a subscription tier, or a significant change in data privacy—the publisher will typically issue an updated EULA requiring the user to click "Accept" upon their next login or software update, rather than rewriting the contract for every routine code release.
