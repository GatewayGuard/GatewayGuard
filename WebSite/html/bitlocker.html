BitLocker Data Encryption

BitLocker scrambles everything on your hard drive so it’s unreadable without your password. If your laptop is lost or stolen, the thief cannot read your files — even if they remove the drive and plug it into another PC.

✓ Checkup walks you through this

What Checkup found and did

Found:Checkup reviews your drive’s encryption. On Windows 11 Home this is called Device Encryption; on Windows 11 Pro it is called BitLocker. Checkup never starts encryption without your explicit permission.
Action taken:Checkup walked you through enabling encryption and saving your recovery key to at least two safe locations, with your approval at each step. This is one of the few settings where Checkup guides you step by step rather than applying the change automatically — because the recovery key is critical and must be saved before encryption begins.

Why this matters

If your laptop is ever stolen, encryption is what protects your files. Without it, anyone with physical access to your drive can read everything on it — your documents, photos, passwords saved in your browser, everything — in minutes, using free tools.

With encryption on, the data on the drive is scrambled. Without your password (or your recovery key), it’s unreadable. The laptop can be wiped and resold. Your data stays private.

How to check it — and turn it on if it is off

The two editions of Windows 11 do this differently. Follow the one that matches your PC. If you do not know which you have, press the Windows key, type winver and press Enter — the box that opens names your edition.

Windows 11 Home

  1. Press the Windows key and type encryption.
  2. Open Device encryption settings.
  3. Look at the Device encryption switch. It should say On. If it says Off, click it to turn it On.
  4. If there is no Device encryption page at all, your PC does not support it. That is a hardware limitation and not something you did wrong — see What to expect below.

Windows 11 Pro

  1. Press the Windows key, type windows security, and open Windows Security.
  2. In the left-hand panel, select Device security.
  3. Scroll down — or use the down arrow key or the Page Down key — and select Manage BitLocker drive encryption.
  4. Look at your C: drive. If it says BitLocker on, there is nothing to do. If it says BitLocker off, click Turn on BitLocker and follow the prompts.
  5. Do not walk away during the next step. Windows will offer to save your recovery key. Save it before you go any further — the section below tells you where.

Your recovery key — do not skip this

Save your recovery key before encryption is complete. The recovery key is a 48-digit number that lets you access your drive if you ever forget your password or if Windows asks for it during startup. If you lose the key and forget your password, your files cannot be recovered — not by Microsoft, not by GatewayGuard, not by anyone.

Save your recovery key in at least two of these places:

  1. Your Microsoft account online — go to account.microsoft.com/devices/recoverykey. If you signed into Windows with a Microsoft account, the key may already be saved here automatically.
  2. A USB drive — save the key file to a USB drive you store somewhere safe (not with the laptop).
  3. Printed on paper — print it and store it somewhere secure, like with important documents.
  4. A password manager — if you use one, paste the key as a secure note.
GatewayGuard showed you this key during setup. If you saved it then, you’re done. If you cannot remember saving it, check your Microsoft account at the link above before doing anything else.

Your backup plan — what to do the day the key is asked for

Saving the key and being able to use it are two different things. The section above is about saving it. This one is about the morning your PC starts up and asks for it, which is the only morning it matters. Read it now, while nothing is wrong.

Print this page, or write the steps on paper and keep them with your key. If the PC is asking for the recovery key, you cannot read this page on that PC. That is the whole problem, and paper is the whole answer.

Before anything goes wrong — both editions

  1. Save the key in two places, and make one of them off the computer. Printed on paper, or on a USB drive kept somewhere else. A copy that lives only on the encrypted drive is no copy at all — you cannot reach it when the drive will not open.
  2. Write down which PC each key belongs to. A 48-digit number with no name on it is not much use if you own two computers.
  3. Tell one other person where the key is. Not the number — where it is kept. If something happens to you, somebody else may need to open that machine.
  4. Check once a year that you can still find it. Put it with something you already do yearly, like your tax paperwork.

On the day the key is asked for

  1. Do not restart repeatedly hoping it clears. It will not, and each restart is more time lost.
  2. Read the Key ID shown on the screen. Windows shows the first part of the key identifier. Match it to the copy you saved, which is how you tell which key it wants if you have more than one.
  3. Fetch your paper or USB copy and type the 48 digits in. There is no dash to type — Windows moves you along on its own.
  4. If you cannot find your copy, go to another device — a phone is fine — and try account.microsoft.com/devices/recoverykey. This works only if the PC was signed in with a Microsoft account and the key was saved there. Sign in with the same Microsoft account that PC uses, not a different one.
  5. If that page is empty and you have no other copy, stop and get a person involved. Do not reinstall Windows and do not let anyone talk you into wiping the drive — that ends any chance of getting the files. See the note below.
The honest part. If the key is genuinely gone and the password is genuinely forgotten, the files cannot be recovered — not by Microsoft, not by GatewayGuard, not by a repair shop, not by anyone. That is not a policy, it is what encryption is. It is also exactly why the four steps above are worth twenty minutes today.

What to expect

Encryption runs in the background and does not slow down your PC during normal use. The initial encryption process (the first time you turn it on) may take 30 minutes to a few hours depending on how much is on your drive — you can use your PC normally while it runs.
